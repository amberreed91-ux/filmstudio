# Film Score & Sound Design

A reference guide for Studio Brain — how music and sound are composed for picture, the vocabulary of film audio, and how sound shapes emotional experience.

-----

## Sound Is Half the Film

George Lucas said that sound is 50% of the moviegoing experience. Walter Murch — the editor and sound designer of *Apocalypse Now* and *The Godfather* — would argue it’s more. The audience can close their eyes and still know where they are emotionally. They cannot close their ears. Sound operates below conscious attention, directly on the body — heart rate, skin conductance, muscle tension. A film with extraordinary sound design and mediocre visuals will feel better than a film with extraordinary visuals and mediocre sound.

-----

## The Sound Hierarchy

### Diegetic Sound

Sound that exists within the world of the film. Characters can hear it. A radio playing. Footsteps. Dialogue. A gunshot. Rain.

### Non-Diegetic Sound

Sound added in post that only the audience hears. The film score. A narrator’s voice-over. The conventional horror sting.

### Source Music (Diegetic Music)

Music that exists in the world — a band playing at a party, a character’s car radio, a jukebox. Characters hear it. The use of source music grounds scenes in reality and can be used ironically (upbeat music during violence, melancholy music during celebration).

### The Crucial Gray Zone

Some of the most sophisticated uses of film audio blur the diegetic/non-diegetic line. Does the character hear the music or not? *Blue Velvet*. *Mulholland Drive*. The sound world becoming unreliable is its own storytelling tool.

## Film Score: The Emotional Architecture

### What a Film Score Does

A film score does not illustrate what we see. At its worst, it does — “mickey-mousing” (a term from animation: the music physically matches every action, like a cartoon). At its best, a score tells the audience what to feel about what they see — often contradicting the surface content to produce a more complex emotional experience.

The score operates on:

- **Emotional amplification:** Intensifying what the image already communicates
- **Emotional counterpoint:** Playing against the image to produce irony, unease, or complexity
- **Narrative context:** Telling the audience something the character doesn’t know yet (the ominous theme before danger arrives)
- **Temporal compression:** Bridging time, smoothing transitions, making a montage feel like a continuous emotional experience

### Leitmotifs

A leitmotif (from German: “leading motif”) is a recurring musical theme associated with a specific character, place, relationship, or idea. When the theme recurs, the association is activated — the audience feels the emotional content of that character or idea even before they appear on screen.

**Classic leitmotif systems:**

*John Williams — Star Wars*

- The Force theme: ascending, warm, inevitable — heroism as destiny
- The Imperial March: descending, mechanical, inevitable — power as violence
- Leia’s theme: lyrical, yearning, fragile — hope as something almost lost

*John Williams — Jaws*

- Two notes. E and F. The simplest possible musical statement of dread. The shark is not the monster — the theme is. The audience fears the music before the monster appears.

*Bernard Herrmann — Vertigo*

- The Prelude’s spiraling motif (drawn from Wagner’s Tristan chord — unresolved harmonic yearning) established obsession as the film’s emotional register before a single image appeared.

*Ennio Morricone — The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*

- Whistled theme, electric guitar, voices, gunshots as percussion. The score made the landscape mythological. Morricone was not scoring a Western — he was inventing the sound of a moral universe.

## The Great Film Composers

### Bernard Herrmann (1911–1975)

**Films:** *Vertigo*, *Psycho*, *North by Northwest*, *Citizen Kane*, *Taxi Driver*
**Signature:** Psychological depth, harmonic unease, the use of unusual instruments (solo strings, bass clarinets), scores that are inseparable from the film’s identity.
**The Psycho strings:** Herrmann wrote the *Psycho* shower scene music for string orchestra only — Hitchcock originally wanted it without music. Herrmann insisted. “Shrill, agonized” was how he described it. The music *is* the murder. Without it, the scene loses half its violence.

### Ennio Morricone (1928–2020)

**Films:** *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*, *Once Upon a Time in the West*, *Cinema Paradiso*, *The Hateful Eight*, *The Mission*
**Signature:** Human voices as instruments, silence as structure, folk and world music elements, scores that exist independently as music (not just functional accompaniment).
**Philosophy:** Morricone composed themes before shooting began — Leone would play them on set during filming to establish emotional tone for the actors.

### John Williams (b. 1932)

**Films:** *Jaws*, *Star Wars*, *Raiders of the Lost Ark*, *E.T.*, *Schindler’s List*, *Saving Private Ryan*, *Catch Me If You Can*
**Signature:** Post-Romantic orchestral grandeur, thematic clarity, leitmotif systems of extraordinary emotional precision.
**The Williams paradox:** His scores are so effective they are sometimes invisible — the emotion they produce is attributed to the film, not the music. *Schindler’s List* without Williams’s violin solo is a different film.

### Hans Zimmer (b. 1957)

**Films:** *The Lion King*, *Inception*, *The Dark Knight*, *Interstellar*, *Gladiator*, *Dune*
**Signature:** Electronic textures blended with orchestra, rhythm as primary driver, the “braaam” (the low-frequency horn blast that became ubiquitous in 2010s trailers).
**Inception’s ticking clock:** The entire score of *Inception* is built on a slowed-down version of Édith Piaf’s “Non, je ne regrette rien” — the same song playing in the film at normal speed. A structural game embedded in the texture of the score itself.

### Jonny Greenwood (b. 1971)

**Films:** *There Will Be Blood*, *The Master*, *Inherent Vice*, *Phantom Thread*, *Spencer*, *The Power of the Dog*
**Signature:** Dissonance, strings used percussively, scores that feel antagonistic rather than supportive — the music as a second film running beneath the first.
**There Will Be Blood:** The opening sequence has no dialogue. Greenwood’s score — jagged, dissonant, Penderecki-influenced — establishes Daniel Plainview’s world as one of violence and obsession before a word is spoken.

### Ryuichi Sakamoto (1952–2023)

**Films:** *Merry Christmas Mr. Lawrence*, *The Last Emperor*, *The Revenant*
**Signature:** Minimalism, piano as primary voice, space between notes as meaning, Eastern and Western synthesis.

### Carter Burwell (b. 1955)

**Films:** Most Coen Brothers films (*Fargo*, *No Country for Old Men*, *True Grit*), *Carol*, *Twilight*, *Three Billboards Outside Ebbing, Missouri*
**Signature:** Understatement, piano and strings used sparingly, knowing when to score and when silence does more.
**No Country for Old Men:** Almost no score. The film uses environmental sound — wind, footsteps, the bolt of a captive bolt pistol — as its sound design. Burwell wrote music that the Coens chose largely not to use. The silence is the score.

## Sound Design as Storytelling

### What Sound Design Is

Sound design is the construction of the entire sonic world of a film — everything that isn’t dialogue or music. Footsteps, weather, room tone, object sounds, weapons, vehicles, crowds, nature, the specific texture of every environment. The sound designer builds a world.

### Walter Murch and the Principle of “Worldizing”

Murch (who coined the term “sound design”) developed the practice of “worldizing” — playing back recorded sounds through speakers in real physical spaces and re-recording them to give them the acoustic character of an environment. Sound recorded in a studio sounds like a studio. Sound re-recorded in a room sounds like it belongs in a room.

### The Hierarchy of Sound

Murch proposed that audiences unconsciously process sound in this order of engagement:

1. **Emotion** — the feeling the sound produces
1. **Idea** — the concept the sound represents
1. **Rhythm** — the temporal pattern of the sound
1. **Space** — where the sound is coming from
1. **Character** — whose sound it is
1. **Content** — what the literal sound means

Sound design that works at the top of this hierarchy (emotion first) is more powerful than sound design that works at the bottom (literal matching).

### Silence as Sound

The most powerful moment in many films is the removal of all sound. *Saving Private Ryan*‘s opening sequence uses a temporary loss of all sound — and a strange muffled quality — to simulate the perceptual shock of combat. The audience experiences the dissociation. *Dunkirk*. *A Quiet Place*. Silence is not the absence of sound. It is a sound event that the audience’s body registers as threat, awe, or grief.

## Temp Tracks and Their Danger

A **temp track** is placeholder music cut into a rough edit before the composer writes the actual score. Directors and editors use temp tracks to feel the emotional rhythm of a film during editing.

**The danger:** Directors fall in love with the temp track. The composer is then asked to write something “just like this” — leading to either plagiarism or a pale imitation. Stanley Kubrick solved this problem by using his temp tracks as the actual score (*2001: A Space Odyssey* — Strauss, Ligeti, Khachaturian).

**Famous temp track situations:**

- *Star Wars*: John Williams partly based his score on the temp track (which used Holst’s *The Planets*). The result was an intentional stylistic conversation.
- *Apocalypse Now*: The Doors’ “The End” in the temp track became the actual opening — sometimes the temp is right.

## Music as Irony and Counterpoint

The most sophisticated uses of music in cinema play against the image rather than with it.

**Stanley Kubrick** was the master: “Singin’ in the Rain” during the rape sequence in *A Clockwork Orange*. “The Blue Danube” during the beautiful, violent choreography of orbital docking in *2001*. “We’ll Meet Again” over nuclear annihilation in *Dr. Strangelove*. The gap between cheerful music and horrifying image produces something neither could produce alone: moral horror with an absurdist face.

**Quentin Tarantino** curates source music with deliberate anachronism. Surf rock in the 1960s. Soul music in medieval Japan. The wrongness of the music against the image produces a vertiginous, satirical effect — history made strange.

**Paul Thomas Anderson**: Jonny Greenwood’s scores for PTA films function as a separate film — aggressive, modern, angular — running underneath period settings. The friction is intentional.

## Sound Vocabulary Reference

|Term        |Definition                                                                                 |
|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Foley       |Custom sound effects recorded in post to match on-screen action (footsteps, cloth, objects)|
|ADR         |Automated Dialogue Replacement — re-recording dialogue in a studio after filming           |
|Room tone   |The ambient sound of a location recorded for use in editing gaps                           |
|Score       |Original music composed for the film                                                       |
|Soundtrack  |All audio in the film; or commercially, the music released from the film                   |
|Source music|Music that exists within the film’s world                                                  |
|Underscoring|Music placed beneath dialogue to support emotional tone                                    |
|Sting       |A brief, sharp musical accent — often used for horror reveals                              |
|Swell       |Music that builds in volume and intensity toward an emotional peak                         |
|Sound bridge|Audio from the next scene that begins over the image of the current scene                  |
|Infrasound  |Sub-20Hz frequencies below human hearing threshold — felt physically, producing unease     |
|Atmos       |The ambient sound layer of an environment                                                  |

## Studio Brain Application

When specifying sound and score for a scene:

- **Diegetic or non-diegetic?** Does the character hear this sound?
- **Score or silence?** Sometimes the most powerful choice is no music. Name what silence does here.
- **Emotional function:** Amplify / counterpoint / foreshadow / ground
- **Instrument/texture reference:** Orchestral warmth / electronic dissonance / solo piano / strings / percussion-forward / voice
- **Composer reference:** Invoke a composer’s grammar (*“Herrmann-style strings”*, *“Greenwood-style dissonance”*, *“Morricone-style silence punctuated by voice”*)
- **Silence moment:** Where does sound drop out entirely, and what does that removal communicate?

**Example:** *“The scene plays without score. The only sounds are the hum of the refrigerator, her breathing, and the sound of the rain outside. When he walks in, the refrigerator stops. The silence is louder than anything the score could say.”*
