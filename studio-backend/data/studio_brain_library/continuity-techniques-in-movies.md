# Continuity Techniques in Movies

A reference guide for Studio Brain — how films maintain (and deliberately break) continuity across scenes and sequences.

-----

## What Is Continuity?

Continuity is the illusion that a film exists in a coherent, consistent world across time. Because films are shot out of sequence — sometimes over months, across multiple locations — every visible detail must match between cuts: eyeline, costume, prop placement, lighting direction, actor position, sound environment. The audience never notices continuity when it works. They only notice when it breaks.

There are two philosophies: **continuity editing** (Hollywood classical style — invisible, seamless) and **discontinuity editing** (art cinema, avant-garde — rupture as meaning).

-----

## Continuity Editing: The Classical System

### Match Cut

The most fundamental continuity tool. An action begun in one shot is completed in another. The cut is hidden inside the motion.

**Types:**

- **Action match cut:** A hand reaches for a door handle in close-up; the next shot shows the door opening from outside.
- **Eyeline match:** Character A looks screen left. Cut to what they’re looking at. The spatial logic is maintained.
- **Graphic match:** Two visually similar shapes or compositions cut together. Kubrick’s bone-to-spaceship in *2001* is the most famous example — a match cut across thousands of years.

**Rule:** The action must overlap slightly between shots so the cut can be placed inside the motion, not at the beginning or end.

-----

### The 180-Degree Rule (The Line)

The fundamental spatial law of continuity cinema.

**How it works:** Draw an imaginary line through the action (two characters talking, a car moving). All cameras must stay on the same side of that line. If you cross it, characters appear to switch positions, audiences lose spatial orientation.

**When to break it:** Intentional disorientation. *The Shining* crosses the line in the hedge maze to make the space feel impossible. Breaking the line is a tool — use it consciously or it reads as error.

-----

### Shot/Reverse Shot

The dominant grammar of dialogue scenes. Character A faces screen right; cut to Character B facing screen left. They appear to be looking at each other. The conversation is assembled from two camera positions.

**The over-the-shoulder variation:** The listening character’s shoulder anchors the frame, maintaining spatial relationship while keeping the speaking character centered.

-----

### Establishing Shot → Coverage

Classical continuity builds scenes in layers:

1. **Establishing shot:** The geography of the space. Where are we? Where is everyone?
1. **Master shot:** The whole scene played wide. Everything happens here.
1. **Coverage:** Medium shots, close-ups, inserts. Cut together in the edit to control rhythm and emphasis.

The editor can then assemble these layers to shape pacing — wide for calm, close for intensity.

-----

### Screen Direction

Characters and objects must move consistently across the frame relative to each other. A character running left to right must continue running left to right unless the film explicitly establishes a directional change.

**Classic use:** In chase sequences, the pursuer runs left-to-right; the person being chased runs right-to-left. They appear to be converging. Reversing this reads as pursuit turning to escape.

-----

### The Cutaway

A shot of something other than the main action that provides context, relief, or information. Also used to cover a continuity error — cut to a reaction shot long enough that the edit hides a costume error or repositioning.

## On-Set Continuity Management

### The Script Supervisor

The person responsible for all continuity on set. Their job:

- Record exactly what actors are wearing, how props are positioned, which hand holds which object, the state of food and drink in every take.
- Match eyeline heights, actor positions, dialogue variants.
- Flag anything that will create an edit problem.

A bad script supervisor is invisible until post-production, when editors discover they have no usable coverage for a scene because the actor’s coffee cup switched hands between takes.

### Continuity Notes and Polaroids

On-set photos of each setup for reference. Now often digital, but the function is the same: a record of the physical state of the scene at the moment of shooting.

### The 30-Degree Rule

When cutting between two shots of the same subject, the camera must move at least 30 degrees, or the cut will read as a jump cut. A slight repositioning reads as an error; a significant repositioning reads as a purposeful new angle.

-----

## Sound Continuity

### Room Tone

Every location has an ambient sound signature. During production, the sound mixer records “room tone” — 60 seconds of silence in the location — so editors can fill gaps between dialogue takes without jarring dead silence.

### Dialogue Continuity

Dialogue must match between takes for editing to work. If an actor says “I never loved you” in the master and “I never loved him” in the close-up, you may not be able to cut between them. This is why script supervisors track every line variant.

### Sound Bridge

Audio from the next scene begins over the image of the current scene (L-cut), or audio from the current scene continues over the image of the next (J-cut). Creates fluid transitions that soften the visual cut.

-----

## Deliberate Continuity Breaks

### The Jump Cut

A cut within a scene that violates continuity — the subject jumps position without apparent motion. Godard used it in *Breathless* (1960) to create disorientation and temporal compression.

**Used for:** Time passing within a scene, psychological fragmentation, stylistic punctuation.

### Temporal Discontinuity

Scenes that refuse to obey chronological logic. Tarkovsky, Resnais, and Chris Marker built entire films from temporal disassembly. *Hiroshima Mon Amour* (1959) intercuts present and past without signaling which is which.

### The Smash Cut

A sudden, jarring cut to a completely different scene, sound environment, or tone. Used for comedic punctuation or dramatic shock. *No Country for Old Men*’s final cut to black is a version of this at feature length.

### The Match Cut as Metaphor (Not Continuity)

Kubrick’s bone-to-spaceship. The match cut repurposed as conceptual leap rather than smooth continuity. The two shots have no diegetic relationship — only visual or thematic rhyme.

-----

## Digital Era Continuity Challenges

### VFX Integration

Every VFX element must match the lighting direction, shadow quality, and camera characteristics of the live-action plate it’s compositing into. Continuity of physics.

### Color and Exposure Matching

Scenes shot on different days, in different weather, with different camera settings must match in color grade. The DI (Digital Intermediate) process corrects for these discrepancies.

### Continuity in Non-Linear Narratives

Films like *Pulp Fiction*, *Memento*, and *Arrival* deliberately use continuity as a puzzle rather than a rule. The audience’s ability to track discontinuity becomes the point of engagement.

-----

## Studio Brain Application

When generating scene sequences, consider:

- **Spatial logic:** Establish where characters are before cutting between them.
- **Time passage:** Name the technique (match cut, jump cut, sound bridge, dissolve) to signal to the director how the edit should work.
- **Continuity as storytelling:** A deliberate continuity break is a signal to the audience. Use it when the story needs disruption, not when you simply need to transition.
- **Script Supervisor’s perspective:** For every scene, note any continuity element that will matter in post — costume, prop state, actor position.
