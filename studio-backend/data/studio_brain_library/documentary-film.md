# Documentary Film

A reference guide for Studio Brain — the modes of documentary, the ethics of filming reality, and how documentary technique increasingly shapes narrative cinema.

-----

## What Documentary Cinema Is

Documentary film is the cinema of the real — or rather, the cinema that claims a relationship to reality that fiction film does not. This claim is the source of both documentary’s power and its ethical complexity. Because the subjects are real people and the events actually happened, every choice the filmmaker makes — what to film, what to exclude, how to edit, whether to use music — carries moral weight that fiction filmmaking does not.

Documentary is also the oldest form of cinema. The Lumières’ actualities — workers leaving a factory, a train arriving — were documentaries before the word existed. The impulse to point a camera at the world and preserve what it sees predates the impulse to construct fictional stories for the camera.

> “All filmmaking is documentary. Even the most fantastical fiction film is a document of its actors and sets.” — Jean-Luc Godard

## The Six Modes of Documentary (Bill Nichols)

Film theorist Bill Nichols identified six documentary modes — approaches to representing reality that each make different claims about truth and each produce different relationships with the audience.

### 1. The Expository Mode

**What it is:** The classic “documentary” most people imagine. A narrator (voice of God) presents information directly to the audience. Evidence is assembled to support an argument. The filmmaker’s position is clear.

**Characteristics:** Voice-over narration, archival footage, expert interviews, music that supports the argument’s emotional tone.

**Examples:** Ken Burns’s historical documentaries (*The Civil War*, *Baseball*, *Jazz*), nature documentaries, most political documentaries.

**The authority question:** The expository mode presents itself as objective while making a specific argument. The narrator’s authority is assumed rather than established. The audience is positioned to receive information, not to question the frame.

### 2. The Observational Mode

**What it is:** The camera observes without intervention — no narration, no interviews, no direct acknowledgment of the camera’s presence. “Cinema Vérité” in its purest form. The filmmaker is a fly on the wall.

**Characteristics:** Handheld, observational camera; synchronized sound; no music added in post; the impression that the camera simply recorded what was there.

**Examples:** Frederick Wiseman’s institutional documentaries (*High School*, 1968; *Hospital*, 1970; *Near Death*, 1989), the Maysles Brothers’ *Grey Gardens* (1975), *Don’t Look Back* (1967, D.A. Pennebaker).

**The impossibility question:** Pure observation is impossible. The camera’s presence changes what is filmed. Subjects perform for the camera, or ignore it with effort, or something in between. The observational mode’s claim to neutrality is always partially false — but the aspiration shapes what is filmed and how.

### 3. The Participatory Mode

**What it is:** The filmmaker is visible in the film — asking questions, reacting, becoming part of the events being documented. The relationship between filmmaker and subject is foregrounded.

**Characteristics:** On-camera interviews, the filmmaker’s questions audible or visible, the filmmaker’s presence acknowledged by subjects.

**Examples:** Michael Moore’s films (*Roger & Me*, 1989; *Bowling for Columbine*, 2002; *Fahrenheit 9/11*, 2004), Werner Herzog’s documentaries, Errol Morris’s interview-driven films.

**The ethics question:** The participatory mode foregrounds the filmmaker’s subjectivity, which is more honest than the expository mode’s false objectivity — but it also raises questions about manipulation, staging, and the filmmaker’s power over subjects.

### 4. The Reflexive Mode

**What it is:** The documentary examines its own processes — the conditions of its making, the filmmaker’s relationship to the subject, the impossibility of documentary truth. The form questions itself.

**Characteristics:** Filmmakers discuss their methods, subjects discuss being filmed, the apparatus of documentary is made visible.

**Examples:** *Man with a Movie Camera* (Vertov, 1929), *Chronicle of a Summer* (Rouch/Morin, 1960), *F for Fake* (Welles, 1973), *Stories We Tell* (Sarah Polley, 2012).

**What it produces:** A more honest relationship to the audience — the documentary admits it is a construction rather than a transparent window on reality.

### 5. The Performative Mode

**What it is:** The filmmaker’s subjective experience is foregrounded. The film uses documentary’s tools (real footage, real people, real events) to explore personal, poetic, or political truth that resists conventional documentary argument.

**Characteristics:** Personal essay, experimental form, the filmmaker’s inner life as subject, associative rather than argumentative structure.

**Examples:** Marlon Riggs’s *Tongues Untied* (1989), Agnes Varda’s *The Gleaners and I* (2000), Chris Marker’s *Sans Soleil* (1983), RaMell Ross’s *Hale County This Morning, This Evening* (2018).

**What it produces:** Documentary as poetry. Not argument but impression. Not information but experience.

### 6. The Poetic Mode

**What it is:** Documentary that prioritizes aesthetic and emotional impression over information or argument. The world is organized by rhythm, juxtaposition, and image rather than by logical argument.

**Characteristics:** Non-chronological, non-argumentative; images assembled by association rather than causation; often no narration.

**Examples:** Dziga Vertov’s *Man with a Movie Camera* (1929), Godfrey Reggio’s *Koyaanisqatsi* (1982), Ron Fricke’s *Baraka* (1992), Victor Kossakovsky’s *Aquarela* (2018).

## The Ethics of Documentary

### The Filmmaker’s Power

The documentary filmmaker has enormous power over their subjects. They decide:

- What to film and what not to film
- How to frame subjects (literally and figuratively)
- What to include in the edit and what to exclude
- How to use music to shape emotional response
- What context to provide or withhold

This power differential — the filmmaker who controls the representation vs. the subject who is represented — is the central ethical challenge of documentary filmmaking.

### Consent and Collaboration

At minimum, ethical documentary requires informed consent from subjects — they must understand what the film is, how it will be used, and what risks their participation entails. But consent is complicated: subjects may consent without fully understanding the implications, the film may change during production, and power imbalances between filmmaker and subject may compromise the genuineness of consent.

**The participatory turn:** Many contemporary documentary filmmakers work collaboratively with subjects — sharing footage, inviting editorial input, sometimes giving subjects creative control. This collaboration produces different (and sometimes more honest) films than the traditional filmmaker-as-expert model.

### The Ethics of Recreations

When events cannot be filmed because they occurred in the past, documentarians sometimes recreate them — staging events with the original participants or with actors. This is ethically contested:

- Does the audience know they’re watching a recreation?
- Does the recreation accurately represent what happened?
- Does staging give the filmmaker undue control over the audience’s perception of events?

**Errol Morris and the interrotron:** Morris developed a device that allows documentary subjects to make direct eye contact with the camera while looking at the filmmaker’s face. The result is more intimate than the conventional interview — but it also raises questions about the filmmaker’s influence over performance.

### The Exploitation Question

Films about poverty, trauma, and marginalized communities raise the question of who benefits. Does the film serve the community it documents, or does it use that community’s suffering for the filmmaker’s artistic and commercial benefit?

**The *Grey Gardens* question:** Albert and David Maysles filmed the Edith Beales — Jackie Kennedy’s aunt and cousin, living in squalor in their decaying mansion — for weeks. The film is extraordinary. It is also a film about two women in a state of significant mental fragility, made by filmmakers with enormous resources relative to their subjects. The ethics of *Grey Gardens* remain disputed.

## The Great Documentary Filmmakers

### Frederick Wiseman (b. 1930)

The greatest practitioner of the observational mode. Wiseman has spent 50+ years filming American institutions — high schools, hospitals, welfare offices, zoos, legislative bodies — in extended observational films that function as sociological studies.

**Method:** Wiseman shoots massive quantities of footage (often 100:1 ratios), then edits alone over months into films that present institutions as systems — the individuals within them are both victims and perpetuators of the system’s logic.

**Key films:** *Titicut Follies* (1967, banned for 24 years), *High School* (1968), *Hospital* (1970), *Near Death* (1989), *Public Housing* (1997), *Ex Libris: The New York Public Library* (2017), *City Hall* (2020)

### Errol Morris (b. 1948)

Morris reinvented the documentary interview — and proved that documentary could be as formally inventive as fiction film.

*The Thin Blue Line* (1988): An investigation of a wrongful murder conviction that helped free an innocent man from death row. The first documentary to use re-enactments not to illustrate what happened but to examine the impossibility of knowing what happened. Philip Glass’s score. A milestone.

*The Fog of War* (2003): Robert McNamara reflecting on the Vietnam War. The interrotron. Eleven lessons of war delivered by the man who made them.

*The Act of Killing* (2012, Joshua Oppenheimer, produced by Morris): Indonesian death squad leaders re-enact their murders in the style of their favorite Hollywood films. One of the most formally radical and morally disturbing documentaries ever made.

### Werner Herzog (b. 1942)

Herzog’s documentaries are not observational — they are participatory to the point of confrontation. Herzog is always present, always pushing, always looking for the moment when the subject breaks through their own surface.

**Principle:** Herzog seeks “ecstatic truth” — a deeper truth than the surface facts of a situation, accessible only through emotional and formal intensity.

*Grizzly Man* (2005): Timothy Treadwell’s footage of his life among Alaskan grizzly bears, reframed by Herzog’s narration and interviews into a meditation on nature, delusion, and death.

*Cave of Forgotten Dreams* (2010): The Chauvet Cave paintings — 30,000-year-old images — filmed in 3D. Herzog’s narration transforms archaeology into philosophy.

### Agnes Varda (1928–2019)

*(see also Foreign Films file)*
Varda’s documentaries are essays — the camera as thinking instrument, the filmmaker’s subjectivity inseparable from the subject.

*The Gleaners and I* (2000): Shot on a small digital camera, Varda films gleaners — people who gather what others leave behind — and finds in them a philosophy of time, waste, and what survives.

*Faces Places* (2017): With street artist JR, Varda travels through rural France, photographing ordinary people and pasting their images on buildings. A film about art, community, memory, and friendship.

### RaMell Ross (b. 1986)

*Hale County This Morning, This Evening* (2018): A film about Black life in rural Alabama, made over five years. Rejects all expository documentary convention — no narration, no interviews, no argument. Pure observation and poetic image. A sustained act of attention to a community that American documentary has usually seen only as subject for advocacy.

## Documentary Technique in Narrative Cinema

Documentary aesthetics increasingly appear in narrative filmmaking — as stylistic choice, as formal argument, or as an attempt to access documentary’s emotional register (the sense that this is real, this is happening).

### The Mockumentary

Fiction that adopts documentary form — handheld camera, interviews to camera, visible crew, improvised feel — to access humor, satire, or a particular kind of intimacy.

*This Is Spinal Tap* (1984, Rob Reiner): The founding text. A fictional rock band documented with complete seriousness.
*The Office* (UK, 2001): Ricky Gervais’s use of the documentary form to create cringe comedy.
*Best in Show* (2000), *A Mighty Wind* (2003): Christopher Guest’s collaborative mockumentaries.
*What We Do in the Shadows* (2014): Vampires flatmates observed documentary-style.

### The Hybrid Narrative-Documentary

Films that blur the line between fiction and documentary — using real people, real locations, real events, and real documentary conventions within a constructed narrative.

*Beasts of the Southern Wild* (2012, Benh Zeitlin): Shot in real Louisiana bayou communities with non-professional actors. The documentary texture is inseparable from the film’s emotional register.

*Uncle Boonmee Who Can Recall His Past Lives* (2010, Apichatpong Weerasethakul): Uses documentary observation to frame entirely supernatural events — the stillness of the observational camera applied to ghosts, spirits, and past lives.

*I, Daniel Blake* (2016, Ken Loach): Loach’s social realist dramas use documentary aesthetics — location shooting, non-professional supporting actors, improvisation — to achieve documentary emotional truth within a fictional narrative.

### The Documentary Aesthetic as Political Position

Choosing documentary aesthetics in a narrative film is a political decision: a refusal of the polished artifice of conventional cinema, a claim that this story belongs to the documentary tradition of truth-telling about the real world.

The Dardenne Brothers, Kelly Reichardt, and Sean Baker all use documentary aesthetics — handheld, natural light, real locations, non-professional actors — as part of their commitment to the reality of their subjects’ lives.

## Studio Brain Application

When drawing on documentary for narrative work:

- **Mode:** Which documentary mode does this scene invoke? Observational (camera as witness) / participatory (filmmaker present) / reflexive (the act of filming acknowledged) / performative (subjective, poetic)?
- **The ethics position:** What is the power relationship between the camera and what it films? Is the camera exploiting, collaborating, or bearing witness?
- **Texture vs. argument:** Documentary texture (handheld, naturalistic, unpolished) signals realness. Documentary argument (narration, selection, music) signals position. Which does this scene need?
- **The observational long take:** Holding a shot past the point where fiction would cut — letting the subject be present rather than performing. What does this scene reveal if the camera simply watches?
- **The reconstruction question:** Is this scene staging something that happened? If so — does the audience know?

**Example:** *“The camera is across the room, observational. It doesn’t push in. It doesn’t cut to closer angles. It simply watches the family at dinner — the conversation, the silences, the way they pass food without looking at each other. After three minutes, one of them says something that changes everything. The camera still doesn’t move. The scene is the watching, not the event.”*
