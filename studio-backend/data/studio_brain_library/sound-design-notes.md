# Sound Design Notes — Zombie Apocalypse Film

## The Core Sound Concept: The Wrong Quiet
The most powerful sound design choice in this film is **absence**. The world is quieter than it should be. That wrongness is felt before it's understood. 14 months in, the sounds that defined modern life are gone:

**What you no longer hear:**
- Traffic (constant low-frequency urban hum — gone)
- HVAC systems, refrigerator hum, electrical buzz
- Distant aircraft
- Music from neighboring spaces
- Construction
- Crowds

**What has replaced them:**
- Wind
- Animal sounds returning — birds, insects, larger wildlife moving back into urban spaces
- The sound of small groups of people in otherwise empty buildings
- The sounds of manual labor — chopping, hammering, digging — without mechanical backing
- The occasional distant sound whose origin is unknown and possibly threatening

This contrast — the wrong quiet — should be established in the film's first minutes and never let the audience fully forget it.

## Zombie Audio Design

### Signature Sound
Zombies are heard before they're seen. The audio signature should be:
- **Respiratory dysfunction** — labored, wet breathing. Not roaring, not screaming. A sound closer to suffering than aggression.
- **Residual vocalizations** — not language, but the remnants of it. Low, unformed sounds.
- **Footfall** — irregular, dragging. Distinguishable from a living person walking.
- **Mass horde** — a low, undifferentiated sound mass when numbers are large enough. Individual sounds merging into something ambient and terrible.

### The Distance Indicator
Sound mix should be calibrated so the audience can estimate zombie proximity by audio alone. This creates acoustic tension even when zombies aren't on screen:
- Far: ambient horde murmur under scene audio
- Approaching: individual sounds beginning to separate from the mass
- Close: breathing and footfall audible; scene audio muffled against it
- Adjacent: near silence on the actor's part; zombie sounds only

## Score Philosophy

### When to Use Music
Score is earned, not assumed. Default is **no score** — the world's ambient sound carries the scene.

Score enters for:
- Significant emotional beats (not action sequences)
- Transitional moments (end of act, major loss)
- Memory or interiority sequences

**Never score action sequences.** The action edit and ambient sound do the work. Score over action distances the audience from physical stakes.

### Score Texture
When score is used:
- Sparse instrumentation — solo strings, single piano, sparse percussion
- No orchestral swell (this isn't that kind of film)
- Consider diegetic music — a character playing an instrument, a found battery-powered radio — as an alternative to non-diegetic score
- The Drowned World sound palette: instruments slightly detuned, resonance of empty spaces

## Key Sound Design Moments

### Establishing the World (Opening)
First sound sequence should demonstrate the new acoustic world without dialogue. Let the audience orient to what's missing.

### The First Zombie Encounter
Sound-first reveal. Build the audio signature before the visual reveal. The scariest the audio design will ever be, because it's the first time.

### The Death Scene
**No score.** The ambient world continues indifferently. This is the most important sound design choice in the film — the world doesn't care that someone died. That indifference, acoustically rendered, is devastating.

### Moments of Genuine Safety
Brief, precious quiet — not the wrong quiet but actual peace. Wind, birdsong, the sound of people breathing without fear. These moments are audio gifts to the audience and they make the next threat scene hit harder.

## Studio Brain Application
Reference this doc when advising on scene audio, directing notes, or post-production planning. Flag any suggestion that reaches for score as a default emotional tool. When a scene calls for tension without action, consider the sound design solution before the visual one — what the audience hears may be the most economical path to the feeling.
