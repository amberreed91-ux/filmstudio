# Location Atlas — Zombie Apocalypse Film

## Location Design Philosophy
Every location in a post-apocalyptic film carries a **before** and an **after**. The dramatic power comes from the collision between what a place was and what it is now. An abandoned hospital isn't just a dangerous location — it's a monument to the collapse of the systems that were supposed to keep people alive.

Choose locations that mean something thematically, not just logistically.

---

## Location Categories

### The Shelter (Base Location)
The group's current home base. Defines their security level, resource access, and psychological state.

**Questions to answer:**
- How defensible is it? (Perimeter, choke points, escape routes)
- What was it before? (The former function haunts the present use)
- What does it cost to maintain? (Labor, vigilance, noise discipline)
- What's the most likely way it fails?

**Thematic resonance options:**
- Former school: hope, children, learning — now a fortress
- Former church: faith, community, sanctuary — what remains of those values?
- Former big box store: consumerism converted to survival utility
- Farmhouse: self-sufficiency, isolation, the American myth of independence

---

### Urban Ruins
Dense zombie population, high resource value, maximum risk.

**Cinematic properties:**
- Verticality — rooftops as escape routes and observation points
- Acoustic danger — urban canyons amplify sound
- Former commercial density means supply runs are possible but contested
- Human-scale geography becomes alien: blocked streets, collapsed sections, flooded underpasses

**Specific urban locations with dramatic potential:**
- **Hospital/medical facility:** High-value supplies, extremely dangerous, emotionally loaded
- **Police station/armory:** Weapons cache, also former symbol of order now symbolizing its absence
- **Underground (subway, tunnels, parking structures):** Maximum claustrophobia, darkness as resource
- **High-rise:** Vertical isolation, visibility advantage, elevator shafts as death traps

---

### Rural/Suburban
Lower zombie density, lower resource density. The slower, more psychologically pressured survival zone.

**Cinematic properties:**
- Open sight lines (threat visible from distance but also *you're* visible)
- Nature reclaiming — overgrowth, animal return, beauty in collapse
- Isolation makes group fractures more dangerous (nowhere to decompress)
- Agricultural potential — a farm is worth fighting for

---

### Transitional Zones (On the Move)
Road sequences, cross-country travel, supply runs. The film's kinetic gear.

**Cinematic properties:**
- Vehicle interiors as pressure cookers for dialogue
- Roadblocks (natural and human-made) as plot interruption
- The open road as false promise of freedom
- What you pass on the road: abandoned vehicles, evidence of other survivors, the dead

---

## Location as Thematic Statement

| Location | What It Says |
|----------|-------------|
| Hospital | The failure of institutions to protect |
| Church | What people reach for when systems fail |
| School | The generation the apocalypse will shape |
| Mall | The absurdity of what was valued before |
| Family home (abandoned) | The specificity of loss — this particular life |
| Prison | Who we locked away and why; reversal of inside/outside |

## Studio Brain Application
When advising on scene location, always ask what the location meant before and how that meaning complicates or amplifies the scene's dramatic content. Flag location choices that are purely functional without thematic resonance. Reference world-rules.md for zombie density logic per location type.
