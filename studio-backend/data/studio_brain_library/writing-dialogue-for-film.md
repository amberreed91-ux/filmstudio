# Writing Dialogue for Film

A reference guide for Studio Brain — subtext, voice, rhythm, screen dialogue vs. stage dialogue, and the craft of making characters speak like themselves rather than like the writer.

-----

## What Screen Dialogue Is

Screen dialogue is not how people talk. It is a highly compressed, purposeful simulation of how people talk — stripped of filler, shaped into rhythm, loaded with subtext, and structured to advance character and situation simultaneously.

The worst screen dialogue explains what is happening. The best screen dialogue reveals who is speaking while hiding what is actually being said.

Every line of dialogue in a finished film must do at least one of these things:

1. Reveal character
1. Advance the situation
1. Establish relationship dynamics
1. Deliver information the audience needs
1. Create or release tension

If a line does none of these, it can almost always be cut — and the scene is better for it.

-----

## The Foundational Rule: Subtext

**Subtext is what the character means beneath what they say.**

Real human communication operates almost entirely in subtext. We rarely say what we mean directly — we approach it, circle it, hide it in humor or aggression or apparent irrelevance. The gap between what a character says and what they mean is where the audience lives. That gap is drama.

**The Iceberg Principle (Hemingway):** The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. Write the surface; let the depth be felt. A scene about divorce can be written as a conversation about who gets the dog.

### Subtext in Practice

**What they say:** “Nice apartment.”
**What they mean:** I didn’t expect you to have done so well without me. / I hate that you’re doing fine. / I want to stay.

**What they say:** “You seem tired.”
**What they mean:** You look terrible. / I’m worried about you. / I don’t want to have this conversation.

**What they say:** “I hear you’ve been seeing someone.”
**What they mean:** Are you over me? / Is it serious? / I made a mistake.

The surface dialogue is banal. The subtext is the scene. The actor delivers both. The audience reads both. The writer only has to write one.

## Voice: How Characters Sound Like Themselves

Every character in a script should sound like no other character. If you can replace Character A’s dialogue with Character B’s dialogue and it still works, you don’t have two characters — you have one character split into two bodies.

### What Creates Distinct Voice

**Vocabulary:** A character’s word choices reveal education, class, background, and self-image. A character who says “I don’t care for that” vs. one who says “that’s trash” vs. one who says “that’s not quite right” — three different people revealed in three word choices.

**Sentence rhythm:** Short sentences suggest directness, aggression, working-class background, or emotional control under pressure. Long sentences suggest education, anxiety, a need to explain, or a character who thinks out loud.

**What they avoid saying:** A character who never says “I love you” directly, who finds other ways — “drive safe,” “you should eat something” — is more specific and more interesting than one who says everything plainly.

**Register:** Formal vs. informal. Does this character use contractions? Do they speak differently to different people (code-switching as character revelation)? Are they performing a version of themselves?

**Idiosyncracy:** The word a character uses too often. The phrase that is theirs. The habit of speech — the question answered with a question, the joke made in serious moments, the silence where words should be.

### Voice Exercise

Write the same line of information — “she died last Tuesday” — in the voice of five different characters with different backgrounds, relationships to the deceased, and emotional states. Each should sound entirely different while conveying the same fact.

## Subtext Techniques

### The Deflection

A character is asked a direct question and answers a different one — usually the question they wish had been asked, or the one they can answer safely.

> “Do you love her?”
> “She makes the best coffee I’ve ever had.”

### The Pivot

A character appears to answer directly, then pivots to reveal they’re answering something else entirely.

> “Are you leaving?”
> “I was thinking about that drive we took up the coast. Do you remember that?”

### The Interruption

A character stops themselves before completing what they were going to say. The unfinished sentence is sometimes more powerful than the finished one.

> “I just wanted to say — I don’t know. Never mind.”

### The Overly Literal Answer

A character answers the literal surface of a question, deliberately ignoring its actual meaning.

> “Can you pass the salt?”
> “Physically? Yes.”

### The Subject Change

Abrupt subject changes reveal what a character is avoiding. The more abrupt, the more they don’t want to be there.

### The False Agreement

A character agrees with something they don’t agree with. The audience knows. The other character may or may not. Either way, the gap creates tension.

## Screen Dialogue vs. Stage Dialogue

### Stage Dialogue

Stage dialogue carries more of the narrative weight. Audiences are farther from the actors, so the voice must communicate what close-up facial expressions do on screen. Stage dialogue is often more explicitly literary — longer speeches, more overt emotional declaration, language as primary event.

### Screen Dialogue

Screen dialogue is compressed. The camera can show what the word doesn’t need to say. An actor’s expression delivers the subtext; the dialogue only needs to establish the surface. Less is almost always more.

**The rule:** If you can communicate it with an image, cut the dialogue. If you can communicate it with a look, cut the line. If the actor can say it without the word, remove the word.

**Chekhov’s on this:** The best stage dialogue tends toward explicit statement. The best screen dialogue tends toward apparent irrelevance — the meaning lives in what’s underneath.

## Dialogue and Rhythm

Good dialogue has rhythm — a cadence that carries the audience through the scene without their noticing. Bad dialogue is arrhythmic — it catches, stutters, stops the flow.

### Short Exchange Rhythm

The tennis match. Quick volleys. Each line short, punchy, building momentum. Used for conflict, comedy, intellectual sparring, and scenes under time pressure.

> “You’re late.”
> “I’m here.”
> “You were supposed to be here an hour ago.”
> “I’m here now.”
> “That’s not the same thing.”
> “Close enough.”

### Long Speech Rhythm

The aria. One character holds the floor — building an argument, making a confession, delivering an ultimatum. The other character’s silence is as important as the speech. These moments must be earned — the character must have been compressed enough that when they speak, it releases rather than indulges.

### The Rule of Interruption

Real conversations interrupt. Screen dialogue that never interrupts sounds written, not spoken. Characters talk over each other, finish each other’s sentences wrong, cut in before the point is made. The interruption can be as revealing as the completed thought.

### The Pause

Silence inside a dialogue scene is one of its most powerful tools. The pause before the answer. The pause instead of the answer. The look across the table after something has been said that cannot be unsaid. Pauses must be scripted — “beat” in the action line — or they will be rushed by actors and directors who don’t know they’re supposed to be there.

## Common Dialogue Failures

### On-the-Nose

Characters say exactly what they mean, directly, without subtext. The audience is told what to feel rather than allowed to feel it.

> **On-the-nose:** “I’m angry at you because you left me when I needed you most.”
> **Better:** “Your flight was fine, I assume. Good. I’m glad.”

### Maid-and-Butler Dialogue

Characters tell each other things they both already know, for the audience’s benefit.

> **Maid-and-Butler:** “As you know, John, we’ve been partners for fifteen years, ever since we met at the academy in 2009.”
> **Better:** Any scene written with the assumption that we’ll learn what we need through implication, context, and action.

### Thesis Statement Dialogue

A character delivers the theme of the film in a single speech. The film literally explains itself.

> “You know what your problem is? You’ve never learned that love isn’t something you earn — it’s something you give.”

If a character states the theme explicitly, the theme is dead. The theme must be felt, not announced.

### The Expository Dump

A character delivers a large quantity of information in an unbroken speech. Information should be distributed through action and conflict, not deposited in a single scene.

### Everyone Sounds the Same

If you removed the character names, you couldn’t tell who was speaking. A failure of voice.

## The Great Dialogue Writers

### Harold Pinter

The pause and the threat. Pinter’s characters speak in mundane language while the power dynamic beneath the conversation shifts constantly. Silence weaponized. The most terrifying conversations in dramatic literature are about furniture arrangements and tea.

**The Pinter pause:** A moment of silence so loaded with unstated meaning that the audience holds its breath. Stage direction: “Pause.” It does more work than a monologue.

### David Mamet

Profanity as music. Interruption as grammar. Repetition with escalation. Mamet’s characters circle what they want — they approach it through apparently irrelevant conversation that is always, somehow, exactly relevant.

**Mamet’s rule:** Every scene is a negotiation. Every character wants something. Every line either advances or retreats from getting it.

### Quentin Tarantino

Pop culture as vernacular. Extended dialogue that appears to be digression but is always character revelation. Characters who talk around violence until violence becomes inevitable. The pleasure of speech in a world where speech only delays the inevitable.

**The Tarantino conversation:** Long, apparently aimless exchanges (cheeseburgers in Paris, foot massages) that establish character, build tension, and make the violence that follows more disturbing because of how human it made everyone feel.

### Aaron Sorkin

Dialogue as combat. The walk and talk. Interruption as dominance. Characters who are too smart and too fast and who talk about what they do rather than what they feel — because what they do IS what they feel.

### Elmore Leonard

The simplest, most rhythmic crime dialogue in American literature. Characters who never explain themselves. Menace delivered with perfect calm.

**Leonard’s rules for dialogue:** Never use a verb other than “said” to carry dialogue. Never use an adverb to modify “said.” If it sounds like writing, I cut it.

### Greta Gerwig

Authentic young female voice without condescension or sentimentality. Characters who say smart things and stupid things in the same breath. Specificity of generational and class experience rendered through the precise word.

## Dialect, Accent, and Vernacular

Dialect in screenplay is a delicate problem. Written phonetically, it is patronizing and difficult to read. Unwritten, it disappears on the page and must be conveyed by the actor.

**The rule:** Convey dialect through vocabulary, syntax, and rhythm — not spelling. A character who says “I ain’t never done nothing like that before” reads clearly without apostrophes and phonetic rendering. The rhythm and word choice do the work.

**Code-switching:** Characters who speak differently in different contexts are more specific and more interesting than characters with a single voice. The way a character speaks to their mother vs. their boss vs. their lover is biographical information.

## Studio Brain Application

When generating dialogue for a scene, specify:

- **Subtext:** What does each character actually want from this scene? What are they unable to say directly?
- **Voice:** What are the vocal signatures of each character — rhythm, vocabulary, what they avoid saying?
- **Technique:** Which subtext technique fits — deflection, pivot, interruption, false agreement?
- **Rhythm:** Short exchange or long speech? Where are the pauses?
- **What’s not said:** Identify the most important unspoken line in the scene
- **Failure mode to avoid:** Which common dialogue failure is this scene most at risk of?

**Example:** *“The scene is a conversation about dinner. Neither character says the word ‘leaving.’ Neither character says the word ‘marriage.’ One character keeps refilling the other’s glass. The other keeps drinking it. By the end of the scene the bottle is empty and nothing has been resolved and everything has been decided.”*
