# Famous Screenwriters & Techniques

A reference guide for Studio Brain — the writers who shaped narrative cinema and the craft tools they used.

-----

## The Screenwriter’s Unique Challenge

A screenplay is not a novel. It is a blueprint for a collaborative industrial process. Every word on the page must justify its existence by serving something that can be filmed. Description creates a world. Dialogue reveals character while advancing situation. Structure creates expectation, then manages it. The great screenwriters understood: the best screenplay is invisible on screen — the audience experiences the film, not the writing.

## The Foundational Writers

### Billy Wilder (1906–2002)

**Films:** *Sunset Boulevard* (1950), *Some Like It Hot* (1959), *Double Indemnity* (1944), *The Apartment* (1960)
**Writing partner:** I.A.L. Diamond (later work)

**Techniques:**

- **The Wilder Rule:** Enter scenes late, leave early. Never show arrival or departure if you can start mid-conflict.
- **Irony as dramatic architecture:** Characters systematically believe the opposite of the truth.
- **Genre as Trojan horse:** Wrap serious content (suicide, alcoholism, fascism) in genre entertainment.
- **The final line:** Wilder obsessed over last lines. “Nobody’s perfect.” (*Some Like It Hot*) ended an argument about the film’s ending. The last thing the audience hears is the residue they carry out.

-----

### Robert Towne (b. 1934)

**Films:** *Chinatown* (1974), *Shampoo* (1975), *The Last Detail* (1973)

**Techniques:**

- **Research as character discovery:** Towne spent years studying 1930s Los Angeles water politics to write *Chinatown*. Character behavior must be rooted in the real physics of a specific world.
- **The tragic ending with internal logic:** *Chinatown*‘s ending is devastating because it’s the *only* coherent conclusion given what the world of the film is. Towne initially disagreed. Polanski was right. The lesson: genre satisfaction vs. thematic truth in conflict — sometimes truth must win.
- **Subtext as primary text:** Characters almost never say what they mean. Every exchange operates on at least two levels.

-----

### Paddy Chayefsky (1923–1981)

**Films:** *Network* (1976), *Marty* (1955), *The Hospital* (1971)

**Techniques:**

- **Prophetic satire:** *Network* was written in 1976 and accurately described the next 50 years of media. Chayefsky wrote by identifying the existing direction of an institution and accelerating it to its logical extreme.
- **Angry monologue as aria:** “I’m mad as hell and I’m not going to take it anymore.” Big speeches in Chayefsky aren’t showboating — they’re the point where characters can no longer contain what the film has pressurized inside them.
- **Television as mirror:** Chayefsky understood that entertainment industries are more interesting as subject than they are as product.

-----

### Nora Ephron (1941–2012)

**Films:** *When Harry Met Sally* (1989), *Sleepless in Seattle* (1993), *Heartburn* (1986)

**Techniques:**

- **Autobiography as universality:** Ephron wrote from her own experience with the confidence that specific female experience was universal experience — a radical act in 1980s Hollywood.
- **The conversation as plot:** In *When Harry Met Sally*, the plot IS the 12-year conversation. Relationship development through dialogue structure rather than event.
- **Emotional precision:** Ephron said she could write a scene about a piece of pie that made audiences cry. Specificity of detail is the route to universal emotion.

-----

### Paul Schrader (b. 1946)

**Films:** *Taxi Driver* (1976), *Raging Bull* (1980), *Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters* (1985), *First Reformed* (1017)

**Techniques:**

- **The Man in the Room:** Schrader’s recurring protagonist is isolated, alienated, keeping a journal, building toward an act of extreme violence or redemption. A spiritual structure borrowed from Bresson and Dostoevsky.
- **Transcendental Style:** Schrader literally wrote a book on it. Everyday mundanity → disparity → a decisive act. Film as a path to the spiritual through the material.
- **Voice-over as unreliable confession:** Travis Bickle’s journal entries are not reliable narration. They reveal a diseased mind while the images show us something different.

-----

### Charlie Kaufman (b. 1958)

**Films:** *Being John Malkovich* (1999), *Adaptation* (2002), *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004), *Synecdoche, New York* (2008)

**Techniques:**

- **Meta-structure as emotional tool:** *Adaptation* is a film about writing the film you’re watching. The meta layer isn’t cleverness — it’s the only honest way to explore creative failure.
- **The literal metaphor:** What if you could erase a relationship from memory? What if someone could enter another person’s mind? Kaufman takes emotional truths and literalizes them into plot mechanics.
- **Incompleteness as authenticity:** Kaufman’s films refuse clean resolution because clean resolution is dishonest. Life doesn’t close arcs.

-----

### Aaron Sorkin (b. 1961)

**Films/Shows:** *The West Wing*, *A Few Good Men* (1992), *The Social Network* (2010), *Moneyball* (2011)

**Techniques:**

- **Walk and talk:** Characters in motion are characters in conflict. Movement prevents scenes from becoming debates.
- **The A-story/B-story mirror:** Every Sorkin episode/act has a parallel structure where the smaller story illuminates the larger moral question.
- **Dialogue as combat:** No Sorkin character speaks just to communicate information. Every exchange is a contest for dominance, approval, or survival.
- **The idealism underneath the cynicism:** Sorkin’s characters talk like cynics and believe like idealists. That tension is the engine of his drama.

-----

### Phoebe Waller-Bridge (b. 1985)

**Work:** *Fleabag* (stage to screen), *Killing Eve*, *Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny*

**Techniques:**

- **The Break:** Direct address to camera (from *Fleabag*) as an unreliable narrator who thinks she’s in control of the audience’s perception. When the break stops working, we know she’s in real trouble.
- **Comic deflection of trauma:** Characters use humor and performance as defense mechanisms, and the story is about what happens when those defenses fail.
- **Female desire written from the inside:** Waller-Bridge writes female interiority — including sexuality, grief, and competitiveness — without apology or male-gaze filtration.

-----

### Rian Johnson (b. 1973)

**Films:** *Brick* (2005), *The Brothers Bloom* (2008), *Looper* (2012), *Knives Out* (2019)

**Techniques:**

- **Genre as lens:** Every Johnson film uses genre (noir, heist, sci-fi, mystery) to examine something the genre doesn’t normally examine. *Brick* is high school social hierarchy. *Looper* is self-loathing. *Knives Out* is class and inheritance.
- **The midpoint reveal:** Johnson restructures genre expectations by revealing what you thought was the mystery’s core earlier than expected — then pivoting to the real story.
- **World rules as character:** Johnson’s films establish clear internal rules for their worlds and then test characters against those rules.

## Core Screenwriting Techniques

### Three-Act Structure

The dominant Western narrative architecture. Setup (25%), Confrontation (50%), Resolution (25%). Not a formula but a description of how human attention processes narrative: orientation → complication → closure.

### The Save the Cat Beat Sheet (Blake Snyder)

15 precise narrative beats mapped to page count. Prescriptive but useful as diagnostic tool to identify structural voids.

### The Hero’s Journey (Joseph Campbell)

Call → Threshold → Trials → Revelation → Return. A mythological template underlying a vast percentage of cinema. Most powerful when its beats are subverted or gendered differently.

### Scene Construction: Desire, Obstacle, Action

Every scene: a character wants something, meets resistance, and takes action. Scenes without all three elements can often be cut.

### The Inciting Incident

The event that sets the story in motion. Must be specific, irreversible, and create a question the film then spends its running time answering.

### Subtext

What is not said. Characters speak around what they actually want or feel. The audience reads the gap. The gap is the drama.

### Scene Turning Points

Every scene should end differently than it begins — the power dynamic shifts, information changes hands, a relationship transforms. Static scenes are the enemy of momentum.

### Plant and Payoff

Information seeded early returns with meaning later. Audiences experience this as both satisfaction and inevitability — the best payoffs feel like they could only ever have been that.
