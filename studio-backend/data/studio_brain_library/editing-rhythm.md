# Editing Rhythm — Zombie Apocalypse Film

## The Governing Principle
Editing rhythm is **emotional management**. In this film, the goal is to make the audience feel the weight of survival — exhaustion, vigilance, grief — and then deploy tension against that baseline. A jump scare means nothing if the audience is already anxious. Control the baseline; control the impact.

## Baseline Rhythm: The Quiet World
The film's default editing pace is **slow**. Slower than commercial instinct says is comfortable. This is deliberate:
- Longer takes in shelter scenes establish normalcy and psychological safety
- The audience needs to *relax* so tension sequences can *land*
- Silence and stillness are not dead air — they are the emotional cost of survival made visible

**Practical baseline:** 8–15 second average shot duration in non-threat scenes. Let conversations breathe. Let faces hold.

## Tension Escalation: The Threat Rhythm
When threat enters, the edit doesn't immediately cut faster — it **tightens** first:

1. **Sound alert** — something heard; edit holds on the face registering it
2. **Body response** — movement slows or freezes; camera settles
3. **Search** — coverage fragments slightly as attention divides
4. **Confirmation** — cut to the threat; rhythm now takes its cue from the characters' capability

Fast cutting in a threat sequence only works if the audience has been in slow cutting long enough to feel the contrast. Never earn your action edit with action editing.

## Specific Scene Types

### The Supply Run
- Begin slow (preparation, the familiar routine)
- Tighten as they enter unknown territory
- Use **silence cuts** — cut away from sound before it resolves; let the audience fill it
- Pay off tension with either a real encounter or a false alarm (false alarms are dramatically useful — they train the audience's expectation)

### The Conversation (Emotional Scene)
- Stay wide first — establish space and physical relationship
- Move closer as emotional stakes rise
- **Do not cut on dialogue** — cut on reaction. The response to the line is usually more important than the line.
- Silence between lines is edit territory — hold it longer than feels comfortable

### The Action Sequence (Zombie Encounter)
- Cut on movement, not sound
- Keep geography clear — audience must always know where everyone is relative to the threat
- **One long take** somewhere in the sequence — a moment where the camera doesn't cut, just survives
- Aftermath: go slow. Let the adrenaline drain on camera.

### The Death Scene
Do not cut away. Hold through the worst of it. Cutting away is the film protecting the audience from feeling something; this film doesn't do that.

### The Quiet Aftermath
After any major event — threat, loss, decision — **give the scene room to breathe**. A character sitting alone for 30 seconds of screen time is worth more than three scenes of dialogue processing the same event.

## Transitions
- **Match cuts** — connect pre- and post-apocalypse, or interior/exterior, through visual rhyme
- **Sound bridges** — audio from next scene begins over last image of previous; world is continuous, not segmented
- **Hard cuts to silence** — more powerful than fade-to-black for moments of sudden loss

## The Editing Don'ts
- Don't cut to establish shots to fill space — every cut earns something
- Don't match-cut on dialogue — it fragments rather than connects
- Don't use music to tell the audience how to feel during an edit sequence; let the edit do the work

## Studio Brain Application
When advising on scene structure, pacing, or narrative efficiency, reference this doc for rhythm guidance. Flag any suggestion that reaches for fast cutting as a default. When a scene feels long, the question is not "where do we cut?" but "what is the length earning emotionally?" Reference specific scene types above when advising on action or emotional sequences.
