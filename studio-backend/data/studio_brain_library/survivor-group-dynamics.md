# Survivor Group Dynamics

## The Fundamental Tension
Every survivor group is a negotiation between **individual survival instinct** and **collective survival math**. The group is safer together. The group is also the source of every interpersonal conflict, resource dispute, and moral compromise in the film. The zombie threat is external. The drama is internal.

## Trust Architecture
Trust in a survivor group is **earned through demonstrated reliability** under pressure, not through likability or history. This creates interesting friction:
- Someone the protagonist loves may not be trusted by the group
- Someone abrasive may be the most trusted person in a crisis
- Trust can be revoked instantly — one concealed bite changes everything

**Trust is also resource-indexed.** When supplies are abundant, people are generous. As supplies deplete, trust calcifies around core relationships. Map your group's resource status to their social temperature.

## Leadership Dynamics
Post-apocalypse leadership is rarely democratic and never stable. Models:
- **Competence leadership:** The person who knows how to do the most things. Respected but not necessarily liked. Exhausting to be.
- **Charismatic leadership:** Someone people follow because the alternative is despair. Fragile — one wrong call shatters it.
- **Inherited leadership:** Leader died; someone has to go next. May not want it. May be wrong for it.
- **Contested leadership:** Two people with legitimate claims. The actual drama engine of many survivor stories.

## Moral Code Fracture Points
The group's collective moral code erodes under specific pressures:

| Pressure | Fracture |
|----------|---------|
| Scarcity | "We can't feed everyone" decisions |
| Threat of outsiders | Paranoia justifying cruelty to strangers |
| Concealed information | Someone knows something that would change the group's calculus |
| Grief | Someone's judgment impaired by recent loss |
| Boredom/safety | Moral transgressions emerge when the immediate threat recedes |

## New Member Integration
Every new addition to the group is a stress test:
- What do they bring? (Resources, skills, information, bodies)
- What do they cost? (Food, trust bandwidth, potential threat)
- Who vouches for them, and what does that cost the voucher if it goes wrong?

The most interesting new member dynamics: the person the group accepts who shouldn't have been, and the person they reject who turns out to have been legitimate.

## Conflict That Isn't About Zombies
The best conflict in this genre is mundane by pre-apocalypse standards:
- Who does the dishes (i.e., who carries their weight)
- Who gets the last of something
- An affair, a secret, a lie told before trust was established
- Someone's pre-apocalypse life bleeding into their present behavior in destructive ways

**Zombies are not the source of drama. People are.**

## Group Fragmentation Patterns
Groups don't usually explode — they erode. Watch for:
1. Small exclusions (not including someone in a decision)
2. Private conversations that create factions
3. A grievance that gets suppressed rather than resolved
4. A leader making a unilateral call that costs someone else

## Studio Brain Application
When advising on group scenes, always ask: where is each person in the trust hierarchy right now, and what would it take to shift it? Flag any conflict that reads as plot-convenient rather than behaviorally motivated. Reference resource status and timeline when calibrating social temperature.
