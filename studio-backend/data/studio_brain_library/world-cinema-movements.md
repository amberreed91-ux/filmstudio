# World Cinema Movements

A reference guide for Studio Brain — Brazilian Cinema Novo, Hong Kong action cinema, the Indian New Wave, Nollywood, and the global movements that expanded what cinema could be and who it could be for.

-----

## Why These Movements Matter

The canon of world cinema taught in most film schools is overwhelmingly European and American. But cinema is a global art form, and some of its most radical and vital movements emerged from the Global South — from countries working with minimal resources, under political censorship, in the aftermath of colonialism, and in response to specific historical crises that shaped both what these filmmakers wanted to say and what formal tools they developed to say it.

These movements are not footnotes to the European New Waves. They are parallel traditions — sometimes directly influenced by European cinema, sometimes developing in explicit reaction against it, always shaped by specific cultural and political contexts that European cinema could not produce.

## Brazilian Cinema Novo (1960s–1970s)

### The Context

Brazil in the 1960s: extreme inequality, military dictatorship after the 1964 coup, urban poverty (favelas), and the rural northeast (sertão) as a landscape of drought, starvation, and feudal economics. Cinema Novo emerged as a response to this reality — and to the Hollywood films and European art films that were the dominant reference points for Brazilian cinema at the time.

### The Manifesto: “An Aesthetics of Hunger”

In 1965, filmmaker Glauber Rocha published “An Aesthetics of Hunger” — the defining statement of Cinema Novo’s philosophy:

> “A camera in hand and an idea in the head.”

The manifesto argued that Brazilian cinema’s poverty — its lack of resources compared to Hollywood — was not a deficiency but a creative and political condition. The hunger of Brazilian reality should be the hunger of Brazilian cinema. Cheap, rough, direct, angry filmmaking in response to an unjust world. The camera as weapon.

This phrase became the defining principle not just of Cinema Novo but of a global tradition of low-budget politically committed filmmaking.

### The Filmmakers

**Glauber Rocha** (1939–1981)
*Barravento* (1962), *Black God, White Devil* (1964), *Land in Anguish* (1967), *Antonio das Mortes* (1969)
The movement’s most radical voice. Rocha’s films are visually violent, politically furious, and formally experimental — mixing documentary and fiction, indigenous Brazilian mythology with Marxist politics, spectacular landscapes with extreme human suffering. His images are unforgettable: the flagellants of *Black God, White Devil*, the operatic violence of *Antonio das Mortes*.

**Nelson Pereira dos Santos** (1928–2018)
*Rio 40 Degrees* (1955), *Barren Lives* (1963), *How Tasty Was My Little Frenchman* (1971)
The elder statesman of Cinema Novo. *Barren Lives* (*Vidas Secas*), based on Graciliano Ramos’s novel, is the movement’s humanist masterpiece — a family of northeasterners in desperate migration, the landscape itself an antagonist. Influenced by Italian Neorealism but entirely Brazilian in its specificity.

**Ruy Guerra** (b. 1931)
*The Guns* (1964), *The Gods and the Dead* (1970)
A Mozambican filmmaker working in Brazil. *The Guns* is a study of institutional violence in the northeast — soldiers stationed to protect a warehouse of food while the surrounding population starves. The moral argument is structural, not melodramatic.

### Cinema Novo’s Visual Signatures

- Harsh natural light — the blazing northeastern sun as moral force
- Non-professional actors in real locations
- Documentary-influenced handheld camera
- Brazilian popular music (especially baião) as political and cultural assertion
- The sertão (northeastern backlands) as allegorical landscape
- Mythological and political content inseparable

### Cinema Novo’s Legacy

Cinema Novo directly influenced the subsequent generation of Brazilian filmmakers — including Fernando Meirelles (*City of God*, 2002) and Walter Salles (*Central Station*, 1998, *The Motorcycle Diaries*, 2004). The “aesthetics of hunger” as principle extended into global cinema: films made with minimal resources about people with minimal resources, in which the formal conditions of production mirror the conditions of the subject.

## Hong Kong Action Cinema (1970s–1990s)

### The Context

Hong Kong in the 1970s: a British colony with a booming film industry, a working-class Chinese audience hungry for stories that reflected their lives, and martial arts as a cultural tradition that cinema could translate into spectacular form. What emerged was some of the most kinetically inventive genre cinema ever made — and an aesthetic that fundamentally changed global action filmmaking.

### Bruce Lee and the Foundation

Bruce Lee (1940–1973) was not primarily a filmmaker, but his four complete films — *Fist of Fury* (1972), *Way of the Dragon* (1972), *Enter the Dragon* (1973) — created the international market for Hong Kong action cinema and established its visual grammar: the long shot that kept the full body visible to honor the athlete’s achievement, the choreographed fight as performance, the martial artist as moral philosopher.

Lee died at 32. What he started became an industry.

### The Shaw Brothers Studio

The defining production house of Hong Kong cinema from the 1960s through 1980s. Director **Chang Cheh** and cinematographer **Kung Mu-to** developed the wuxia (martial arts/swordsmanship) film as a form — operatic violence, male brotherhood, honor codes, and the spectacular body as visual subject.

### Jackie Chan and the Comedy-Action Synthesis

Chan (b. 1954) reinvented Hong Kong action cinema by fusing martial arts with physical comedy — explicitly citing Buster Keaton as his primary influence. Chan’s films (*Drunken Master*, 1978; *Police Story*, 1985; *Project A*, 1983) treat the human body as a comic instrument as well as a martial one. His elaborate stunt sequences — performed without doubles, injuries documented in the end credits — made his body the film’s subject.

**Chan’s camera philosophy:** Show the stunt completely, from the widest angle that captures the full achievement. Do not cut within a sequence in ways that obscure what the performer is actually doing. Trust is the contract with the audience.

### John Woo and Heroic Bloodshed

John Woo (b. 1946) developed the “heroic bloodshed” genre — crime films elevated to operatic tragedy. His signature: slow motion, dual-wielding pistols, doves, Catholicism, male bonds forged through violence and tested by betrayal, action sequences choreographed with the logic of dance.

**Key films:** *A Better Tomorrow* (1986), *The Killer* (1989), *Hard Boiled* (1992)

*Hard Boiled*‘s hospital sequence — a continuous action sequence shot across multiple days that follows Chow Yun-fat and Tony Leung through corridors, stairwells, and wards — is one of cinema’s greatest sustained action set pieces.

Woo’s Hollywood films (*Face/Off*, *Mission: Impossible 2*) brought his aesthetic to American cinema — but his Hong Kong films remain the definitive expression of his vision.

### Wong Kar-wai and Art Cinema

Wong Kar-wai (b. 1958) used Hong Kong’s genre filmmaking infrastructure to make art cinema of extraordinary sensory richness. While Woo was making action films, Wong was making films about time, memory, and longing — but with the same cinematographer (Christopher Doyle), the same handheld intimacy, the same urban geography.

*(See Foreign Films file for fuller coverage of Wong’s work)*

### The Legacy: Hollywood Imports Hong Kong

The influence of Hong Kong action cinema on Hollywood is immeasurable. The Wachowskis hired Hong Kong choreographer Yuen Woo-ping to design the action sequences for *The Matrix* (1999). Tarantino’s *Kill Bill* is an explicit love letter to Hong Kong martial arts cinema. The editing rhythms, camera positions, and choreographic logic of contemporary action cinema are largely derived from Hong Kong.

## The Indian New Wave / Parallel Cinema (1960s–1990s)

### The Context

Indian cinema in the early 20th century developed into two distinct traditions: the enormously popular commercial cinema (which would become Bollywood) and a smaller tradition of socially engaged, aesthetically ambitious filmmaking influenced by Italian Neorealism and the European New Waves. The Indian New Wave — also called Parallel Cinema — was this second tradition.

### Satyajit Ray (1921–1992)

Ray is the central figure of Indian art cinema — and one of the greatest filmmakers who ever lived.

**The Apu Trilogy:** *Pather Panchali* (1955), *Aparajito* (1956), *The World of Apu* (1959). Shot over years with borrowed equipment, non-professional actors, and no studio support. A boy’s life from childhood through young adulthood, set in rural Bengal. The influence of Renoir (whose *The River* Ray had worked on) and Italian Neorealism is visible — but the film is entirely Bengali, entirely specific, entirely itself.

**Ray’s subsequent work:** *The Music Room* (1958), *Charulata* (1964), *The Chess Players* (1977), *Ganashatru* (1989). A complete body of work that rivals any filmmaker of his generation.

Akira Kurosawa said: “Not to have seen the cinema of Ray means existing in the world without seeing the sun or the moon.”

### Ritwik Ghatak (1925–1976)

Where Ray’s films are controlled, Ghatak’s are raw — melodramatic, operatic, politically furious. His trilogy on the Partition of Bengal (*Meghe Dhaka Tara*, 1960; *Komal Gandhar*, 1961; *Subarnarekha*, 1965) is a sustained howl of grief at the political violence that divided a people. Less internationally celebrated than Ray; increasingly recognized as equally significant.

### Mrinal Sen (1923–2018)

Sen’s films engaged more directly with Marxist politics than Ray’s — *Bhuvan Shome* (1969), *Akaler Sandhane* (1980), *Kharij* (1982). His style is deliberately rough, influenced by the French New Wave’s rejection of classical polish.

### Mani Kaul (1944–2011) and Kumar Shahani (b. 1940)

The most formally radical Indian filmmakers. Kaul’s *Uski Roti* (1969) — a woman waiting for her husband on a roadside — uses extreme duration and reduction (static shots, minimal narrative event) to explore time, waiting, and female experience. Influenced by Bresson and the Indian classical arts (raga structure applied to film duration).

### The Contemporary: Adoor Gopalakrishnan and Girish Kasaravalli

The Parallel Cinema tradition continued in regional language filmmaking — especially Malayalam (Kerala) and Kannada (Karnataka) cinema — when Hindi-language parallel cinema declined in the 1990s as Bollywood’s commercial success crowded it out.

## Nollywood: The Nigerian Film Industry

### What Nollywood Is

Nigeria’s film industry — Nollywood — is the second-largest film industry in the world by volume, producing approximately 2,500 films per year (compared to Hollywood’s approximately 700). Since its emergence in the early 1990s, it has become the dominant cultural force in sub-Saharan African cinema and a significant part of the African diaspora cultural economy globally.

Nollywood’s founding moment: *Living in Bondage* (1992), a direct-to-video drama that sold approximately 750,000 VHS cassettes in Nigeria. The direct-to-video model — bypassing the theatrical infrastructure that Nigeria lacked — became the industry’s defining distribution format.

### Nollywood’s Characteristics

**Volume and speed:** Nollywood films are produced fast and cheap — budgets often under $20,000, shoots of 1–2 weeks. The economic model prioritizes output over individual production quality.

**Genre:** Nollywood has developed its own genre conventions. Spiritual warfare films (Lagos prosperity gospel Christianity vs. juju/witchcraft as narrative conflict). Family melodrama (inheritance, marriage, in-law conflict). Romantic comedy. Crime thriller. The spiritual warfare genre — films about demonic attack, prayer as weapon, faith as the only defense against supernatural threat — has no direct equivalent in Western cinema.

**Language:** Films are produced in English, Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, and other Nigerian languages. The Yoruba-language film tradition predates “Nollywood” itself — traveling theater groups adapted their stage performances to video in the 1980s.

**Distribution:** Initially VHS, then DVD sold through markets. The rise of iROKOtv (streaming) and Netflix’s investment in Nigerian content have created theatrical and global streaming distribution alongside the traditional market.

### Nollywood’s Evolution: New Nollywood

The term “New Nollywood” emerged in the 2010s to describe higher-budget, more technically polished Nigerian productions aimed at theatrical release and international distribution.

**Kunle Afolabi** (*Figurine*, 2009; *Phone Swap*, 2012): The filmmaker most associated with New Nollywood’s theatrical ambitions.

**Kemi Adetiba** (*The Wedding Party*, 2016; *King of Boys*, 2018): *The Wedding Party* became the highest-grossing Nigerian film in history. *King of Boys* is a political crime film — a woman’s ascent through Lagos’s criminal underworld — of genuine sophistication.

**Netflix and Nollywood:** Netflix’s investment in Nigerian content (*Lionheart*, 2018 — the first Netflix original film from Nigeria; *Blood Sisters*, 2022) has brought Nollywood to global audiences and raised the resources available to Nigerian filmmakers.

### Why Nollywood Matters

Nollywood is the clearest demonstration that cinema does not require Hollywood infrastructure to reach massive audiences. It developed its own production model, its own distribution system, its own genre conventions, and its own aesthetic from scratch — driven by audience demand rather than institutional gatekeeping. A billion-dollar industry built on $20,000 budgets and street market distribution.

## The Romanian New Wave (2000s–present)

### The Context

Romania after the fall of Communism in 1989: a society processing decades of authoritarian trauma, institutional corruption, poverty, and the specific moral compromises required to survive under Ceaușescu. The Romanian New Wave filmmakers — almost all graduates of the Bucharest Film Academy — developed a cinema of remarkable formal discipline to examine this history and its aftermath.

### Characteristics

- Long takes — extremely long, often in single locations, with multiple characters
- Real time or near-real time sequences that refuse to cut away from difficulty
- Social realism without sentimentality
- Communist-era trauma as subject — directly or obliquely
- Minimal music — often no score at all
- An aversion to conventional narrative resolution

### Key Filmmakers

**Cristian Mungiu** (b. 1968)
*4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days* (2007) — Palme d’Or, Cannes. A woman helping her roommate obtain an illegal abortion in Communist Romania. Shot in long takes, in real locations, with almost no score. The film’s most devastating scene — the protagonist waiting in a hotel restaurant while her roommate is in a back room — is held for what feels like an eternity. The waiting is the subject.

*Beyond the Hills* (2012), *Graduation* (2016), *R.M.N.* (2022) — a sustained body of work examining post-Communist Romanian society with the same formal discipline.

**Cristi Puiu** (b. 1967)
*The Death of Mr. Lazarescu* (2005) — a sick elderly man passed between hospitals in a single night, each institution more indifferent than the last. 150 minutes. One of the definitive films about bureaucratic indifference and the dissolution of social solidarity.

**Corneliu Porumboiu** (b. 1975)
*12:08 East of Bucharest* (2006), *Police, Adjective* (2009) — films about language, authority, and the meaning of words in a society where the official vocabulary and lived reality have always been different.

## Studio Brain Application

When drawing on these movements:

- **Cinema Novo:** Films made with minimal resources about people with minimal resources. The camera as weapon. Political anger as aesthetic principle. The landscape as moral universe.
- **Hong Kong action:** The full body visible. Choreography as cinema. The stunt as trust contract with the audience. Male bonds forged in violence.
- **Indian Parallel Cinema:** Duration as meaning. The specific weight of a face or a landscape. Grief for historical rupture. Ray’s control vs. Ghatak’s operatic excess.
- **Nollywood:** Speed, volume, and directness. The spiritual warfare genre as psychological realism. The diaspora audience as subject and market.
- **Romanian New Wave:** The long take held past comfort. Real time as ethical position. The institutional indifference. No resolution because the situation has no resolution.

**Example:** *“The scene is shot in one take — six minutes, unbroken. Two characters in a waiting room. The camera is across the room, observing. Neither character knows the scene is about their relationship — they think it’s about the form they need to fill out. By minute four the form is still blank. By minute six nothing has happened and everything has changed.”*
