# Production Design — Zombie Apocalypse Film

## Design Philosophy
The world is 14 months into collapse. The initial chaos phase is over. What remains is **adaptive decay** — humans have been living inside this broken world long enough to develop workarounds, routines, and a vernacular aesthetic of survival. Nothing is pristine. Nothing is picturesque. Everything has a use-history.

The production design question is always: **what did people do with what was left?**

## The Decay Timeline
14 months means:

**What's gone:**
- Fresh produce, packaged goods past expiration
- Functioning vehicles (fuel degradation, mechanical failure)
- Most electronics (batteries dead, no charging infrastructure)
- Glass in most urban windows (broken in initial conflict or weather)
- Consistent lighting in any structure

**What remains:**
- Canned goods (approaching end of practical shelf life — creates urgency)
- Mechanical tools, hand tools
- Fabric, clothing (repaired, layered, functional over aesthetic)
- Books, paper
- Solar panels (functional if undamaged; rare and valuable)
- Bicycles (increasingly the primary personal transport)

**What's new (survivor-built):**
- Repaired perimeters (wire, wood, salvaged fencing — imperfect)
- Improvised water collection systems
- Hand-cultivated garden patches
- Crude signage and marking systems between survivor communities
- Bartered goods showing trade networks (object from far away signals someone traveled)

## The Shelter Aesthetic
A lived-in survivor shelter reads as:
- **Layered occupation** — multiple uses for every surface; sleeping, storage, and work happen in the same zone
- **Organized scarcity** — supplies carefully arranged, counted, rationed; abundance is conspicuous by its absence
- **Personalization under pressure** — small objects of pre-apocalypse life (photographs, a child's drawing, a book) amid purely functional gear. These are the emotional hot spots of the set.
- **Improvised light** — candles, lanterns, flashlights mounted to surfaces. Light sources are *precious* and *positioned*.
- **Noise dampening** — fabric hung over surfaces to reduce sound transmission; survival instinct become interior design

## Costume & Character Design
Clothing communicates:

| Character State | Clothing Signal |
|----------------|----------------|
| Established survivor | Layered, repaired, functional; worn in specific ways that signal competence |
| Newcomer | Less adapted; still wearing remnants of before-life that aren't practical |
| Leadership | Often carries more (supplies, tools); the physical weight of responsibility |
| Deteriorating character | Maintenance of appearance breaking down |

**No one wears clean clothes.** Dirt, wear, repair are baseline. Cleanliness is a luxury — a character who manages to clean themselves signals either resources or a psychological need for control.

## Props & Objects
High-value props in this world:
- **Medications** (antibiotics especially — life or death commodity)
- **Batteries and solar chargers**
- **Maps** (paper; GPS is dead)
- **Radios** (communication with outside)
- **Seeds** (agricultural future)
- **Weapons** (firearms limited by ammunition; bladed and blunt weapons increasingly primary)

Objects that carry emotional weight:
- Pre-apocalypse photographs
- Children's items (especially if the child is gone)
- Objects representing former identity (a professional tool, a sports item, a musical instrument)
- Handwriting — notes, journals, lists — evidence of specific people who may no longer exist

## Studio Brain Application
Use this doc when advising on scene staging, prop selection, and visual storytelling through objects. Flag any production design suggestion that reads as too clean, too stylized, or inconsistent with the 14-month decay timeline. When a scene calls for an emotional object, reference the emotional weight category above.
