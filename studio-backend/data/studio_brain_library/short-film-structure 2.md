# Short Film Structure — Zombie Apocalypse

## The Short Film Constraint
A short film is not a feature with scenes cut out. It is a **single dramatic argument** — one question, one pressure point, one emotional transaction between the audience and the film. Everything in the short exists to execute that one thing with maximum precision.

For a zombie survival short, the constraint is also an opportunity: you can skip world-building setup because the audience already carries genre knowledge. You can begin in the middle of the situation and trust them to follow.

## The One-Scene Principle
The most effective zombie shorts are built around **one scene under pressure** with a clear before/after:
- Before: a stable (if fragile) situation
- Pressure: an event that forces a decision or reveals a truth
- After: the situation permanently changed, and we understand the cost

The world-building, the backstory, the group dynamics — all of it exists in implication, not exposition.

## Structure Options

### Option 1: The Incident (10–15 min)
A single event and its immediate aftermath. One location, small cast, compressed timeframe.
- **Open:** Group in established routine — we read their relationships quickly through behavior
- **Incident:** Something goes wrong (a bite, a breach, a stranger, a hard choice)
- **Crisis:** The incident forces a decision with no clean answer
- **Aftermath:** The decision made, its cost visible, no resolution — just continuation

### Option 2: The Mission (12–20 min)
Characters leave safety for a specific purpose; the mission reveals something.
- **Open:** The mission established — what they're going for, why, what's at stake
- **Out:** Journey to objective; tension builds; character dynamics established in motion
- **The Turn:** Something unexpected at the objective changes the mission's meaning
- **Return (or failure to return):** Resolution of the objective question; character revelation

### Option 3: The Arrival (10–15 min)
A newcomer enters an established group. The integration process as dramatic engine.
- **Open:** Established group glimpsed; their dynamics implied
- **Arrival:** The newcomer and their immediate reception
- **Pressure:** Something forces a trust decision faster than the group is comfortable with
- **Resolution:** Accepted, rejected, or something more complicated — and what that reveals about the group

## Compression Techniques

### Environment as Exposition
Don't explain the world — show a detail that implies it. One shot of a hand-drawn map on a wall tells us more than a character saying "it's been 14 months since the outbreak."

### Behavior Over Dialogue
The established survivors move through their shelter with practiced efficiency. We read their competence — and their exhaustion — without anyone explaining it.

### The Loaded Object
One prop that carries emotional history — a photograph, a child's shoe, a wedding ring worn on a cord around the neck. The audience provides the backstory; the film provides the image.

### Implied Off-Screen History
A look between two characters. A hesitation before answering a question. A scar that no one comments on. The film's compression comes from what it trusts the audience to feel without being told.

## What NOT to Do in a Short
- **Don't open with backstory.** Begin in the situation.
- **Don't introduce more characters than the story can pay off.** Two to four is the practical range.
- **Don't resolve everything.** Survival stories don't resolve; they continue. End on a shift, not a conclusion.
- **Don't use voiceover to explain what the images should show.**
- **Don't withhold the emotional payoff.** Short films can't afford to be coy; the emotional transaction must complete.

## The Last Image
The final image carries disproportionate weight in a short. It should:
- Land on a face, not an action
- Contain ambiguity — survival is ongoing, not triumphant
- Leave the audience with a feeling, not a question to resolve

## Studio Brain Application
When advising on structure, identify which option above this film is executing and calibrate advice accordingly. Flag any structural suggestion that introduces unnecessary complexity or delays the central dramatic question. For scenes in development, always ask: what is this scene's single dramatic purpose, and is everything in it serving that purpose?
