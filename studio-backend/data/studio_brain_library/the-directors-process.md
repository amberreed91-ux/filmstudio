# The Director’s Process

A reference guide for Studio Brain — pre-production, shot lists, storyboards, blocking, working on set, and the day-to-day grammar of actually directing a film.

-----

## What a Director Actually Does

The director is the single person responsible for the unified vision of a film — from the first reading of the script through the final color grade. Every other creative department (cinematography, production design, costume, sound, editing, acting) answers to the director. The director answers to the producer — and, ultimately, to the film itself.

The director’s job is not to be the most technically expert person on set. It is to hold the vision clearly enough that every collaborator can serve it — and to create the conditions in which the best work can happen.

> “The director must be a dictator who serves the vision, and a servant who leads the people.” — no single attribution; a principle universally understood

-----

## Phase One: Development and Script Analysis

### Reading the Script as a Director

A director reads a script differently than a producer or actor. They are asking:

- **What is this film about — beneath the story?** The theme that the narrative is a vehicle for.
- **What is the emotional experience the audience should have?** Scene by scene, act by act.
- **What does this look like?** Every scene translated into visual terms — light, space, camera position, movement.
- **What does this sound like?** The sonic world — music, silence, ambient sound, the texture of the audio environment.
- **What is the film’s visual metaphor?** The image or image system that externalizes the theme.
- **Where are the scenes that will make or break the film?** The sequences that must be extraordinary for the film to work.

### The Director’s Treatment

Before production begins, directors often write a treatment — a document that translates the script into visual and tonal terms. Not a shot list; a vision document. What the film looks like, what it feels like, what references it draws from, what the world sounds like, what the character arc looks like in visual terms.

The treatment is a communication tool — it tells collaborators (the DP, the production designer, the composer) what world they are all building together.

## Phase Two: Pre-Production

Pre-production is where the film is made — or unmade. It typically runs 6–12 weeks for a studio film, 2–4 weeks for an independent. Every decision made in pre-production saves time and money on set. Every decision left for the set costs both.

### Assembling the Key Crew

The director’s first practical decision: who are the key collaborators?

**The DP (Director of Photography):** The visual collaborator. The director chooses a DP whose aesthetic sensibility aligns with the film’s needs — not necessarily the best DP, but the right one. The relationship must support genuine creative dialogue.

**The Production Designer:** The builder of the world. Must understand the film’s visual metaphor and be able to translate it into physical space.

**The Editor:** Many directors attach an editor before shooting begins — because the editor’s understanding of what the film is trying to do will shape how coverage is designed. The editor is the director’s collaborator in what the film becomes in post.

**The Casting Director:** The person who finds the actors. One of the most consequential decisions in any film — the wrong cast makes the right vision impossible.

### Location Scouting

The director and production designer visit potential locations. Every location is evaluated on:

- Does it serve the scene’s emotional requirements?
- Does it serve the cinematographer’s lighting approach?
- Is it practically manageable (access, noise, logistics)?
- Does it feel like it belongs to this film’s world?

Many directors find that locations give them things that no set could provide — accidents of architecture, quality of light, the specific texture of a real space — that then inform how they shoot the scene.

### Casting

The director works with the casting director to find the actors. This process — auditions, chemistry reads, callbacks — is among the most consequential in the entire production. Casting well means that a significant portion of the director’s work is already done; the actor brings what the role needs. Casting badly creates problems that no amount of on-set work can solve.

**Chemistry reads:** When casting an ensemble or a pair, the director must verify that the actors’ dynamic produces what the film needs — not just individual competence but relational truth.

### Concept and Reference Development

The director develops a visual reference package with the DP and production designer — paintings, photographs, other films, architectural images — that articulates the film’s visual language before a camera rolls.

**The mood board:** A visual document of the film’s palette, texture, lighting quality, compositional style.

**The reference film list:** Films that share qualities the director wants — not to copy, but to communicate. “The light should feel like Lubezki in *The Tree of Life*. The compositions should have the stillness of Ozu. The color palette should be as controlled as Wes Anderson but without the symmetry.”

## Phase Three: Shot Lists and Storyboards

### The Shot List

A shot list is a document specifying every shot the director intends to capture, organized by scene. For each shot:

- Shot number and scene number
- Shot type (EWS, WS, MS, MCU, CU, ECU, insert)
- Camera movement (static, pan, tilt, dolly, handheld, Steadicam, crane)
- Lens (approximate focal length or quality — wide, normal, telephoto)
- Brief description of action in frame
- Any special notes

The shot list serves multiple functions:

- It forces the director to think through every scene visually before arriving on set
- It gives the DP time to prepare lighting and equipment
- It tells the AD (Assistant Director) how long each setup will take
- It is a tool for scheduling — which shots can share a setup, what order minimizes lighting changes

**The shot list as flexibility:** The best directors know their shot lists but are not imprisoned by them. The list is a plan; the set is where the plan meets reality. When an actor does something unexpected, or the light is wrong, or the location doesn’t match the imagination — the director must be able to adapt.

### Storyboards

Storyboards are drawn illustrations of the film’s shots — a comic-book version of the film before it’s shot. They communicate:

- Exact composition of each frame
- Camera angle and height
- The visual relationship between shots (how they will cut together)
- Complex action sequences (how the geography of a chase or fight works shot by shot)

**When storyboards are essential:**

- Complex action sequences or visual effects shots where every element must be planned precisely
- Sequences that will be assembled from many short shots (Eisenstein-style montage)
- Sequences where the director must communicate precise visual requirements to a large crew
- Animation (every frame is storyboarded before production begins)

**When storyboards are limiting:**

- Character-driven drama where the performance must be discovered
- Scenes in real locations where the space will inform the approach
- Directors who think through the camera, not before it

Many great directors never storyboard. Kubrick storyboarded obsessively. Cassavetes never did. The choice reflects a philosophy about where the film lives — in the preparation or in the moment.

### The Shotgun Approach vs. the Coverage Approach

**Coverage:** Shooting a master (wide shot of the full scene), then shooting every piece of coverage needed to assemble the scene in the edit — medium shots, close-ups, reaction shots, inserts. The editor has maximum flexibility. The director has maximum insurance.

**The designed approach:** Shooting only what the film needs — a specific selection of shots that will be assembled in a specific way. No coverage that won’t be used. The film exists as conceived, not as assembled from options.

The coverage approach is safer and slower. The designed approach is faster, more specific, and more likely to produce the film the director imagined — or an unwatchable one if the imagination was wrong.

## Phase Four: On Set

### The Shooting Day

A standard shooting day runs 12 hours (call to wrap). The day’s work is specified in the call sheet — produced by the Assistant Director — which lists every scene to be shot, the cast and crew required, the location and time, and the order of shooting.

**The first AD:** The director’s operational partner on set. The AD manages the schedule, the crew, the logistics of the day. The director must be freed from operational concerns to think about the film. A great first AD protects the director’s attention.

**The rhythm of a setup:** The director arrives at a new setup. They look at the space, position the actors, work through the blocking with the actors and DP. The DP and gaffer light the setup. The camera is positioned and tested. The actors rehearse. They roll.

**The golden hour:** On a 12-hour day, there are perhaps 4 hours of genuine creative focus — when the director’s attention is fully on the scene and the performance. The rest is logistics, waiting for light, technical preparation, moving equipment. The director’s job is to protect those 4 hours.

### Blocking

Blocking is the placement and movement of actors within the scene — where they stand, when they move, how they relate to the physical space.

**The director’s approach to blocking:**

Some directors arrive with blocking predetermined — they know exactly where each actor will be and when they will move. The actors execute the plan.

Other directors let the actors inhabit the space first — they watch the actors find their natural positions and movements, then adjust. This approach often produces more organic blocking because the movement arises from the actors’ genuine sense of the scene rather than an external plan.

**The relationship between blocking and camera:** The camera must be positioned to serve the blocking — but the blocking can also be designed to serve the camera. Directors who think in both directions simultaneously produce scenes where movement and camera feel unified rather than composed separately.

**The walk-through:** Before every setup, the director walks the actors through the scene — usually without cameras, without the crew watching — to establish the blocking and give notes. This protects the actor’s process from the mechanical pressure of the set.

### Working with Actors on Set

*(See Acting for the Camera file for actor methodology detail)*

**The one-note principle:** Between takes, one note. The director identifies the most important thing that needs to change and communicates it specifically. Multiple notes overwhelm. One note — the right one — can transform a take.

**The whispered note:** Many directors give notes privately, between themselves and the actor, so that the correction doesn’t happen publicly in front of the crew. This protects the actor’s dignity and often produces more genuine responses.

**Protecting the actor from the crew:** A large film set — 50, 100, 200 people — is an intimidating environment for a performer who is trying to be emotionally open. The director’s job includes managing the set’s energy to create conditions of safety.

**Multiple takes and their uses:**

- **Take 1:** The actor’s instinct — often the purest, sometimes not yet at the scene’s full depth
- **Take 2–3:** The actor has found something; refining
- **Take 4–6:** The territory of exploration — trying different approaches
- **Take 7+:** Either breakthrough or exhaustion; the director must read which

**The “print it” decision:** After each take, the director decides whether to print (keep) that take. “That’s a print” means the take is usable. The director is assembling a set of options for the editor.

### Coverage and the Axis

As the director moves through the coverage of a scene — from master to medium to close-up — they must maintain the 180-degree axis (see Cinematography file) and maintain consistency in performance across setups. An actor who does something specific in the master must do the equivalent thing in the close-up, or the editor cannot cut between them.

**The continuity of performance:** The script supervisor tracks performance details as well as physical continuity — which hand held the glass, which line had a particular delivery. Their notes protect the editor’s ability to cut.

## Phase Five: Post-Production

### The Director and the Editor

The director’s relationship with the editor is the film’s second major creative collaboration — equal in importance to the director-DP relationship, and in some ways more intimate. The editor sees every frame of footage. They often know what the film is before the director does.

**The director’s cut:** After principal photography, the editor assembles a rough cut — often called the editor’s cut. The director then works with the editor through multiple passes to shape the film: the rough cut, the fine cut, the director’s cut (the version the director considers complete), and then (on studio films) negotiations with the studio over the final cut.

**The picture lock:** The point at which the edit is finalized — no more changes to the visual cut. After picture lock, the film goes to color grading, sound mixing, and music scoring.

### The Director and the Composer

The director communicates the emotional arc of the film to the composer — what the score should do and, crucially, what it should not do. The score is not decoration; it is the film’s emotional grammar.

**Spotting session:** The director and composer watch the film together and mark where music should begin and end (“spot” the music). Decisions about what the score should feel like, what it should avoid, what emotional register it should operate in.

**The temp track:** The editor has almost certainly used temporary music during editing. The composer must navigate the director’s attachment to the temp while finding an original solution. Many directors become so attached to temp tracks that they hamstring their composers.

### The Color Grade

The director and DP work with the colorist in a DI (Digital Intermediate) session — adjusting the color, contrast, and exposure of every shot to achieve the film’s final visual identity. This is where the cinematography file’s color philosophy becomes a practical reality.

## The Director’s Vision: What It Actually Means

“Vision” is not mystical. It is the ability to hold a clear, specific idea of what the film should feel like — in every moment, across every department — and to communicate that idea with enough precision that every collaborator can serve it.

The director who has vision knows, when they see a take, whether it is right or wrong — and can say why. They know when a lighting setup is wrong and what it should be. They know when the production design is too much or not enough. They know when the editing is cutting too fast and when it’s too slow.

This knowledge comes from having done the work before shooting begins — having thought through every scene, every shot, every moment, until the film exists in the imagination with enough clarity that reality can be tested against it.

## Studio Brain Application

When generating scenes with directorial specificity:

- **Pre-production thinking:** What is the visual metaphor for this scene? What reference films/images communicate the desired quality?
- **Shot list thinking:** What specific shots does this scene need? Name them — not generically but specifically: “a Steadicam following shot that starts behind her and moves around to her face as she reaches the door.”
- **Blocking intention:** Where are the characters in the space, and why? What movement, and at what moment?
- **The one note:** If you were giving one direction to the actor for this scene, what would it be?
- **Coverage philosophy:** Is this scene designed (specific shots only, assembled as conceived) or covered (maximum options for the editor)?
- **Post-production anticipation:** How will this scene be cut? What does the editor need from the footage?

**Example:** *“The director has one setup for this scene — a single long take, Steadicam, moving from the doorway through the apartment to the window. No coverage. The actors must play it completely because there is nothing to cut to. The one note: ‘Take twice as long as you think you need to cross the room. The camera will find you.’ The scene is designed. It will work or it won’t. That’s the bet.”*
